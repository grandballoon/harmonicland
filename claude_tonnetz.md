## The core object

The Tonnetz is a **quotient of a lattice**. Start with the free abelian group on two generators — you're picking two intervals as basis vectors. Euler's choice: perfect fifth (7 semitones) and major third (4 semitones). So a pitch class is

$$p = 7a + 4b \pmod{12}, \quad (a,b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$$

That map $\phi: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}/12$ is a surjective group homomorphism. Its **kernel** is what makes tessellation "work": the set of $(a,b)$ that return you to the same pitch class. Solve $7a + 4b \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$ and you get a rank-2 sublattice generated by, e.g., $(0,3)$ (three major thirds = octave) and $(4,-1)$ (four fifths = major third, up an octave). So the Tonnetz-as-drawn is $\mathbb{Z}^2 / \ker\phi$, a torus with 12 vertices. That's the answer to "why can I tile forever in any direction": you're drawing the universal cover $\mathbb{Z}^2$, and the repetition you see is the deck transformation group $\ker\phi$ acting on it.

**Any starting note** works because $\mathbb{Z}/12$ is homogeneous — translation by any pitch class is an automorphism of the whole structure. Nothing distinguishes C. Choosing C is a choice of origin, i.e., of an affine structure on what is really a torsor.

## Why fifths and thirds specifically

They're not sacred. Any pair $(u,v)$ with $\gcd$ conditions making $\langle u,v\rangle = \mathbb{Z}/12$ generates *a* Tonnetz. Fifths+thirds is chosen because the third basis direction, $u - v = 3$ (minor third), closes triangles whose vertices are exactly major and minor triads. That's the payoff: **triads become faces of a simplicial complex**, not arbitrary sets.

## Where the group theory enters

Two groups act, and it matters which one you mean:

- **$T/I$ group** (order 24): transpositions and inversions, $\cong D_{12}$. Acts on the 24 major/minor triads simply transitively.
- **PLR group** (Neo-Riemannian): generated by three involutions on triads — Parallel, Leittonwechsel, Relative — each of which flips a triangle across one of its edges. Also $\cong D_{12}$, and it is the **centralizer** of $T/I$ in $\mathrm{Sym}(24)$. This duality (Lewin's) is the reason PLR is the "geometric" group: PLR moves are precisely edge-flips in the Tonnetz triangulation, so voice-leading parsimony = adjacency in the complex.

## The generalization you probably want

Replace $\mathbb{Z}/12$ with $\mathbb{Z}/n$, replace the pair of generators with any $k$-tuple, and you get **generalized Tonnetze** as quotients of $\mathbb{Z}^k$. Cohn's condition for the classical case (three interval classes summing to 0 mod 12, generating parsimonious voice leading) is the design constraint. The "any direction forever" freedom is exactly the freedom to choose a fundamental domain.

## Sources with real mathematics in them

- **Dmitri Tymoczko, *A Geometry of Music*** (Oxford, 2011) — and his *Science* paper "The Geometry of Musical Chords" (2006). Orbifolds, quotient spaces, actual topology. Closest to your engineering instincts.
- **David Lewin, *Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations* (GMIT)** — the foundational text. Defines a GIS as a set + group + a torsor-like interval function. Dense but rigorous; this is where the whole transformational program starts.
- **Guerino Mazzola, *The Topos of Music*** — category theory and topos-theoretic music. Extremely heavy, but if you want the maximal-generality version, this is it.
- **Richard Cohn, *Audacious Euphony*** (2012) — the clearest treatment of the Tonnetz-as-torus and parsimonious voice leading. Less formalism, more musical payoff.
- **Thomas Noll**, various papers on the "mathematical Tonnetz" and word-theoretic scale theory — very algebraic.
- **Julian Hook, *Exploring Musical Spaces*** (2023) — the modern textbook. Rigorous, systematic, and the best single place to start if you want the whole apparatus built up properly.
- **Journal of Mathematics and Music** — the actual venue; search there for "generalized Tonnetz."

Start with Hook, then Tymoczko. Lewin when you want the axioms.
